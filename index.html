Core banking concepts

Repo rate
Rate at which RBI lends short-term funds to banks against eligible collateral. A higher rate generally makes borrowing costlier.
SDF
Standing Deposit Facility: RBI absorbs liquidity from banks without providing collateral.
CRR
Cash Reserve Ratio: share of a bank’s net demand and time liabilities kept as cash with RBI.
SLR
Statutory Liquidity Ratio: share maintained in liquid assets such as cash, gold and approved securities.
NPA
A loan asset where interest or principal remains overdue beyond the regulatory threshold; for standard term loans, the commonly tested threshold is more than 90 days.
Priority sector
Directed lending categories such as agriculture, MSMEs, education, housing and weaker sections, under RBI norms.

Payments & inclusion

UPI
Instant account-to-account payments using a virtual payment address, mobile number or QR, operated by NPCI.
IMPS
Immediate interbank electronic transfer available round the clock.
NEFT
Electronic funds transfer settled in half-hourly batches and available 24×7.
RTGS
Real-time, transaction-by-transaction gross settlement, mainly for high-value transfers.
AEPS
Aadhaar Enabled Payment System supports basic banking through Aadhaar authentication and business correspondents.
DICGC
Deposit insurance corporation covering eligible deposits in insured banks, subject to the prevailing statutory limit and conditions.

Institutions

RBI
India’s central bank: monetary policy, currency issuance, reserves, payment systems and bank regulation.
NABARD
Apex development financial institution focused on agriculture and rural development.
SIDBI
Principal financial institution for promotion, financing and development of the MSME sector.
SEBI
Regulates India’s securities market and protects investor interests.
IRDAI
Regulates and develops the insurance sector in India.
PFRDA
Regulates and develops the pension sector, including the National Pension System.

Accounts, credit & risk

CASA
Current Account Savings Account deposits; usually a lower-cost funding source for banks than term deposits.
MCLR
Marginal Cost of Funds based Lending Rate, an internal benchmark for certain floating-rate loans.
Credit score
A summary indicator of credit behaviour; lenders also use income, obligations and their own underwriting rules.
Basel norms
Global standards covering bank capital, risk and liquidity. Capital buffers absorb unexpected losses.
KYC
Customer-identification and due-diligence process used to reduce fraud, money laundering and misuse.
Phishing
Fraudulent attempt to obtain credentials through deceptive messages or sites. Banks do not need your PIN or OTP to receive money.

1. Simplification, Approximation & Number Series

1.1 The order of operations — BODMAS without exceptions

Every simplification question in bank prelims is decided by one rule: Brackets → Of/Orders → Division → Multiplication → Addition → Subtraction. Division and multiplication share a rank and are done left to right; so do addition and subtraction. The most punished mistake is doing addition before subtraction out of habit: in 60 − 20 + 10, the answer is 50, not 30.

Worked example: Simplify 120 ÷ 6 × 2 − 18 + 3².
Step 1 — powers: 3² = 9 → 120 ÷ 6 × 2 − 18 + 9.
Step 2 — left to right: 120 ÷ 6 = 20, 20 × 2 = 40 → 40 − 18 + 9.
Step 3 — left to right: 40 − 18 = 22, 22 + 9 = 31. (Doing 18 − 9 first gives the trap answer 27.)

1.2 The memorised bank: fractions, squares, cubes

Prelims rewards recall, not calculation. Memorise cold:

  • Fractions ↔ %: 1/2 = 50%, 1/3 ≈ 33.33%, 1/4 = 25%, 1/5 = 20%, 1/6 ≈ 16.67%, 1/7 ≈ 14.28%, 1/8 = 12.5%, 1/9 ≈ 11.11%, 1/10 = 10%, 1/11 ≈ 9.09%, 1/12 ≈ 8.33%.
  • Squares 1–30 and cubes 1–15. Spots like 17² = 289, 13³ = 2197 and 24² = 576 appear repeatedly.
  • √2 ≈ 1.414, √3 ≈ 1.732, and square roots of 2, 3, 5, 7 to three decimals.

Worked example: 37.5% of 480 + 12.5% of 320 = ?
37.5% = 3/8, so 3/8 × 480 = 180. 12.5% = 1/8, so 1/8 × 320 = 40. Total = 220 — under 15 seconds, no long multiplication.

1.3 Multiplication shortcuts

  • ×5: halve and ×10 → 48 × 5 = 240.
  • ×25: quarter and ×100 → 36 × 25 = 900.
  • ×125: divide by 8 and ×1000 → 48 × 125 = 6000.
  • (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²: 63 × 57 = (60)² − (3)² = 3600 − 9 = 3591.
  • (a ± b)² = a² ± 2ab + b²: 105² = 100² + 2(100)(5) + 5² = 11025.

1.4 Approximation: round with compensation

In approximation questions you round both operands to friendly numbers, then compensate for the direction of the error. The golden rule: round one operand up and the other down when multiplying or dividing, so errors cancel.

Worked example: 498 × 21.8 ≈ ? — 500 × 22 = 11000. But 500 is above 498 and 22 is above 21.8, so both errors push high; true value 10856, and 11000 is fine for options spaced wide. Better: 502 × 19.7 → 500 × 20 = 10000; errors oppose each other, true value 9889.4 — much closer.

Common traps: (a) rounding only one side and trusting the last digit; (b) treating "≈" questions as exact — never compute square roots of ugly numbers fully, e.g. √1225 is exactly 35, but √1288 ≈ 36 works; (c) in percentage-heavy approximation, rounding the percentage and the base both upward compounds the error badly.

1.5 Number series: the pattern library

Attack a series in a fixed order: (1) check first differences; (2) check second differences; (3) check ×/÷ patterns; (4) check squares/cubes ± constant; (5) check alternating (odd/even positions separate); (6) check prime-based or digit operations.

  • Difference series: 7, 12, 19, 28, 39 → differences 5, 7, 9, 11 (odd numbers). Next difference 13 → 52.
  • Second difference: 3, 6, 12, 21, 33 → differences 3, 6, 9, 12; second differences constant at 3. Next difference 15 → 48.
  • Multiply + add: 2, 6, 16, 42, 108 → ×3 − 0, ×3 − 2, ×3 − 6, ×3 − 24… instead see: ×2 + 2, ×2 + 4, ×2 + 10? Cleaner: each = previous × 2 + (previous − 2): verify pattern ×2 + (2, 4, 10, 24)… Classic clean version: 3, 7, 16, 35, 74 → ×2 + 1, ×2 + 2, ×2 + 3, ×2 + 4 → next ×2 + 5 = 153.
  • Squares/cubes ± n: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30 → n² + n. Next: 42 → 56? No: 6² + 6 = 42, next 7² + 7 = 56… careful: series is 1²+1, 2²+2, 3²+3, 4²+4, 5²+5 → next 6²+6 = 42 is wrong ordering — the series 2, 6, 12, 20, 30 gives next 42.
  • Alternating: 5, 8, 13, 20, 29 → differences 3, 5, 7, 9 → next difference 11 → 40.
  • Prime-linked: 4, 9, 25, 49 → squares of primes (2, 3, 5, 7) → next 11² = 121.

Wrong-number series: find the single misfit, usually by testing the strongest pattern — don't force-fit. Trap: in 6, 13, 28, 59, 122 (×2 + 1, ×2 + 2, ×2 + 3, ×2 + 4), the terms are 6, 13, 28, 59, 122 — all fit, no error. The exam version changes one term; test the pattern against every term, not just the first three.

2. Arithmetic: Percentages, Profit-Loss, Interest, Time-Work-Speed

2.1 Percentages: the foundation of everything

Core: x% of y = (x/100) × y. Always identify the base — every percentage question dies or survives on the base.

  • Symmetry trick: x% of y = y% of x. So 32% of 50 = 50% of 32 = 16. Use this when the swap is easier.
  • Successive changes: two changes of a% and b% give a net change of a + b + (a×b)/100 (signs included: increase positive, decrease negative). Example: price rises 20% then falls 20%: net = 20 − 20 + (20×−20)/100 = −4%. Trap: it is never 0%.
  • Percentage points vs percent: a rate rising from 10% to 12% rises by 2 percentage points, which is a 20% relative increase.
  • "A is what % of B" = (A/B) × 100; "A is p% more than B" means A = B(1 + p/100), and B is not p% less than A — B is less by p/(100+p) × 100%. If A is 25% more than B, B is 20% less than A.

Worked example: A town's population grows 10% in year 1 and 20% in year 2. Net growth? Use the formula: 10 + 20 + (10×20)/100 = 32%, not 30%. Extend to three steps by folding two at a time: 32% then 5% more → 32 + 5 + (32×5)/100 = 38.6%.

2.2 Profit and loss

Definitions: CP (cost price), SP (selling price), MP (marked price). Profit/loss percent is always computed on CP unless the question says otherwise.

  • Profit% = (SP − CP)/CP × 100. If profit% = p, SP = CP × (100 + p)/100.
  • Two successive discounts of a% and b% on MP → single equivalent discount = a + b − (a×b)/100. Discounts of 20% and 10%: 20 + 10 − 2 = 28% off, not 30%.
  • Faulty-weight trick: a seller claims no profit but uses a 900 g weight for 1 kg. True profit% = (1000 − 900)/900 × 100 = 11.11%. General: profit% = (error/(true weight − error)) × 100.
  • CP unknown: "A man sells two articles at ₹1200 each, gaining 20% on one and losing 20% on the other." Net loss = (20×20)/100 = 4% of total CP. Total SP = 2400 = 0.96 × total CP → total CP = 2500, loss = ₹100.

Trap: "sold at 25% profit" with SP = ₹500 → CP = 500/1.25 = ₹400. Candidates dividing by 0.75 or multiplying by 0.75 both go wrong — derive from SP = CP(1 + p/100), never from memory of a shortcut.

2.3 Simple and compound interest

  • SI = P × R × T / 100. If SI for 2 years is ₹800 at 10%, P = 800/(0.1×2) = ₹4000.
  • CI = P(1 + R/100)^T − P. Amount A = P(1 + R/100)^T.
  • CI − SI for 2 years = P(R/100)². This single formula solves a whole family: if CI − SI = ₹48 for 2 years at 20%, then P = 48/(0.2)² = ₹1200.
  • CI − SI for 3 years = P(R/100)² × (3 + R/100). At 10%, that's P × 0.01 × 3.1.
  • Half-yearly compounding: halve the rate, double the time. 10% p.a. for 1 year half-yearly → P(1.05)² = 1.1025P: effective 10.25%.
  • Installment trick: a sum becomes ₹x in n equal annual installments at r% — use A = x[1 − (1+r/100)^−n]/(r/100). For 2 years at 10%: each installment = P × 1.21/2.1. Trap: forgetting that each installment earns different interest.

Worked example: ₹5000 at 20% compounded annually for 2 years. Year 1 interest = 1000, year 2 interest = 1200 (on 6000). CI = ₹2200; SI = 2000. Difference = 200 = 5000 × (0.2)² ✓.

2.4 Time and work

Rule: work done per day = 1/(days to finish alone). Combined rate = sum of individual rates; total time = 1/(combined rate).

  • A finishes in 12 days, B in 18: combined rate = 1/12 + 1/18 = 5/36 → together they take 36/5 = 7.2 days.
  • Efficiency method: if A is twice as efficient as B, A takes half the time. Assign units: total work = 36 units (LCM of 12, 18); A does 3/day, B does 2/day; together 5/day → 7.2 days. The unit method kills fraction errors.
  • Alternate days: A and B alternate starting with A. Per 2-day cycle = 5 units. 36 = 7×5 + 1 → 14 days + 1 day (A's turn) = 15 days. Trap: check whose turn the leftover falls on.
  • Pipes: inlet = positive work, outlet = negative. Pipe A fills in 4 h, B empties in 6 h: rate = 1/4 − 1/6 = 1/12 → 12 hours to fill.

2.5 Time, speed and distance

  • D = S × T. Unit hygiene: convert km/h to m/s by ×5/18; 36 km/h = 10 m/s.
  • Equal-distance average speed is the harmonic mean: for speeds a and b over the same distance, average = 2ab/(a + b). 60 and 40 km/h → 48 km/h, not 50.
  • Trains: a train passing a pole covers its own length; passing another train covers the sum of lengths. Time = length/speed with matched units.
  • Relative speed: opposite directions → add; same direction → subtract.
  • Boats: downstream speed = u + v, upstream = u − v (u = boat, v = stream). Solving two equations gives u and v; trap: averaging downstream and upstream speeds gives u, not v.

Worked example: A 240 m train at 72 km/h (20 m/s) crosses a 120 m platform: total distance = 360 m, time = 360/20 = 18 seconds.

3. Data Interpretation

3.1 The method: read before you calculate

DI sets burn time, not because the maths is hard, but because candidates calculate before reading. Follow this order every time:

  1. Read the question stem first — know which values you actually need before touching the data.
  2. Scan headings, units and footnotes — is it ₹ crore or ₹ lakh? tonnes or kg? "Excluding exports"? One footnote hides 40% of DI errors.
  3. Extract only needed numbers — jot them in a column; never carry a five-digit number in your head.
  4. Estimate the answer band — is it ~30% or ~130%? Kill wrong options before computing exactly.
  5. Compute once, cleanly — and only to the precision the options demand.

3.2 The core operations (everything DI reduces to these)

  • Percentage change = (New − Old)/Old × 100. "Growth of exports from 2019 to 2020" uses 2019 as base — candidates repeatedly use the later year.
  • Ratio comparisons without dividing: to compare A/B vs C/D, cross-multiply A×D vs C×B. Bigger product wins. This avoids two long divisions.
  • Averages: sum then divide; for pie charts, total = 100% always — average share = total/n is trivial, so questions ask about weighted or per-capita splits instead.
  • Growth rates over multiple years: CAGR ≈ [(End/Start)^(1/n) − 1]; for prelims, test simple year-on-year first — the question rarely needs the compounded figure.
  • Part-to-whole: share = (part/total) × 100. In pie charts, convert degrees to percent: 1% = 3.6°, so a 72° slice = 20%.

3.3 Set-type playbooks

Tables: number the rows/columns on paper. Most table questions are just "find row, find column, apply one formula". The traps are unit changes mid-table (row 1 in thousands, totals in lakhs) and derived rows (a "profit" row that is revenue minus cost — verify before reusing it).

Bar + line combos: the most common bank mains format. Two series on different scales (left axis = crores, right axis = percent) — check which axis belongs to which series before reading any value.

Pie charts: always note whether the pie shows values or percentages; mixed-pie questions (two pies for two years with different totals) require converting both to absolute values before comparing slices. Trap: a bigger slice in 2021's pie can mean a smaller absolute value if the 2021 total shrank.

Caselets (paragraph DI): convert prose to a table first — underline every number, assign it a row. Caselets are 3–4 questions for the price of one reading; skipping them in prelims is often the right time call.

Missing data / data sufficiency DI: if a value isn't needed, don't derive it. Roughly one question per set is solvable by elimination.

3.4 Worked mini-set

Table: Company X revenue (₹ crore): 2020: 120, 2021: 150, 2022: 135. Q1. Revenue growth 2021 vs 2020 = (150−120)/120 = 25%. Q2. Revenue decline 2022 vs 2021 = (150−135)/150 = 10%. Q3. Average revenue = 405/3 = 135. Note Q2's base is 150, not 120 — the repeating trap.

Pie worked example: total sales ₹720 crore; Product A's slice = 90°. Share = 90/3.6 = 25% → ₹180 crore. If next year's total is ₹900 crore and A's slice shrinks to 72° (20%) — A's absolute sales stay ₹180 crore despite the smaller slice. Never compare slices across pies without converting to values first.

3.5 The five classic traps

  1. Wrong base year in growth questions.
  2. Unit mismatch — crore vs lakh, thousand vs million.
  3. Adding percentages — a 10% rise then 10% fall is a 1% fall, never 0% (Chapter 2).
  4. Reading the wrong axis on dual-axis charts.
  5. Over-computing — if options are 24%, 31%, 42%, 58%, computing to one decimal place is wasted time; bound and pick.

Speed target: a 5-question prelims DI set should fall in 6–8 minutes; if you're past 10, mark and move — mains sets reward accuracy, not heroics on one set.

4. Logical Reasoning

4.1 Alphabet mastery: the EJOTY key

Every coding and series question runs on letter positions. Memorise the EJOTY anchor — E=5, J=10, O=15, T=20, Y=25 — and fill in around it (A=1, D=4, F=6, H=8, M=13, N=14, P=16, R=18, V=22, Z=26). Also memorise the reverse pairs that sum to 27: A–Z, B–Y, C–X, D–W, E–V, F–U, G–T, H–S, I–R, J–Q, K–P, L–O, M–N. Writing A–Z numbers on rough paper at the start of the reasoning section is legitimate and saves minutes.

Worked example: In a code, BANK is written as DCPM. Each letter +2: B→D, A→C, N→P, K→M. So LOAN → N, Q, C, P = NQCP.

4.2 Coding–decoding patterns

Check patterns in this order: (1) fixed shift (each letter ±n); (2) variable shift (+1, +2, +3…); (3) position sums/products (word coded as sum of positions — "DOG" = 4+15+7 = 26); (4) reverse/opposite letters (A↔Z etc.); (5) vowel/consonant rules; (6) substitution codes with sentence pairs ("red is sweet" / "sweet is tasty" → common word "sweet" maps to common code word).

Worked example: If CAT is coded 3120 — C=3, A=1, T=20, concatenated: positions written together. Then DOG = 4, 15, 7 = 4157. Trap: reading it as sum (24) or product.

Number-to-letter codes: given 1=A … 26=Z mappings with multi-digit ambiguity (e.g. "12" = AB or L) — test both readings; the valid word/pattern decides.

4.3 Alphanumeric series

Treat letters and numbers as independent sub-series. In B2, D6, F12, H20 — letters skip one (B, D, F, H), numbers are n(n+1): 1×2, 2×3, 3×4, 4×5 → next 5×6 = 30 with J: J30. Trap: trying to find one rule linking letter and number.

4.4 Direction sense

Draw a compass. Moving left from North = West; from East = North. Two 90° turns in the same direction = reversal; left + right cancel. Worked example: Facing North, turn right, then left, then right: N → E → N → E. Facing East. For distance questions, sketch the path and use Pythagoras on the right triangle (3-4-5 and 5-12-13 appear constantly). Trap: "turns left" describes the person's left, not the page's left — re-anchor at each step.

4.5 Order, ranking and comparison

  • Rank from left + rank from right = total + 1. 7th from left and 5th from right → total = 7 + 5 − 1 = 11.
  • Interchange positions: A and B swap; A's new rank = B's old rank. If A was 4th from left and becomes 9th from left after swapping with B (9th from right originally), total = 4 + 9 − 1… wait — after the swap A occupies B's old seat: A's new rank from left (9) + A's new rank from right (4, = B's old rank from left side) → total = 9 + 4 − 1 = 12. Keep "whose seat is whose" straight.
  • Comparison chains: "A is taller than B but shorter than C" → C > A > B. Minimum info needed to fix a full order = (n − 1) independent comparisons; count them before concluding.

4.6 Odd one out and analogy

Look for: (1) prime vs composite (numbers); (2) vowel/consonant count; (3) position-sum parity; (4) one term breaking a +/− pattern. For analogies, state the relationship in words first — "X is the capital of Y" style — then apply. Trap: the first plausible relation is sometimes wrong; verify it against all options, because analogy setters deliberately plant a second pattern that fits three options.

4.7 Statement–conclusion and cause–effect

A conclusion follows only if it must be true given the statements — not if it is merely plausible. "All dogs are animals. All animals breathe." → "All dogs breathe" follows; "Some dogs are pets" does not. In cause–effect, a shared event can be an effect of a common cause; two effects of one cause are not cause-and-effect of each other.

5. Puzzles & Seating Arrangement

5.1 The universal method

Never hold a puzzle in your head. For every set, draw three things:

  1. Facts: definite statements — "A sits at the left end." Place immediately.
  2. Slots: empty seats/positions/floors drawn as boxes or a circle.
  3. Constraints: relative statements — "B is third to the right of C" — that link two entities.

The anchor rule: start from the most constrained entity (the one mentioned most, or the only absolute position). The branching rule: when a clue allows two placements ("A is next to B"), draw both and kill one with the next clue — never pick one arbitrarily and hope.

5.2 Linear arrangement: the left–right convention

Assume everyone faces North unless told otherwise — then left = your left as you look at the row. "Third to the right of C" counts three seats starting from the seat next to C; C itself is not counted. If the row faces South, left and right flip — this is the single most tested trap.

Worked example: Seven people in a row facing North. "A is at an end. C is third to the right of A." A must be at the left end (if A were at the right end, three seats to its right wouldn't exist). Positions: A=1, C=4. "B is between A and C, two seats from A" → B=3. "D is at the other end" → D=7. Remaining E, F, G take 2, 5, 6 by further clues.

Double row (facing each other): Row 1 faces South, Row 2 faces North. "X faces Y" fixes one opposite pair. Note: when both rows face each other, a person's left in Row 1 is the opposite side from Row 2's left — draw arrows, don't trust intuition.

5.3 Circular arrangement: facing rules

  • All facing the centre: clockwise = left, anticlockwise = right (verify with one person: raise your mental "right hand" toward the centre).
  • All facing outward: clockwise = right, anticlockwise = left.
  • Mixed facing: handle each person individually; "immediate neighbours face opposite directions" creates an alternating chain.

Worked example: 6 around a table, all facing centre. "A is opposite D. B is second to the left of A." Place A at top; left (clockwise) second seat = B. D opposite A (bottom). "C is between B and D" — two arcs; "not adjacent to A" kills one. This is the standard 4-clue circular skeleton — practise until it's mechanical.

Trap: "second to the left" in a circle of n: positions are cyclic, so "immediate right of the last seat" wraps to the first.

5.4 Floor / box / stack puzzles

Draw a vertical stack with the top floor highest-numbered. "Two floors between A and B" means exactly one floor lies between them (A at 5, B at 3, or A at 3, B at 5). "A lives above B but not immediately above" — mark and move on; resolve by elimination. Box puzzles are the same with boxes in a stack: count from the bottom unless the question says otherwise — check the counting direction; half of all errors here come from counting from the top when the set counts from the bottom.

5.5 Scheduling puzzles (day–month–person)

Build a grid: dates/months as rows, attributes as columns. Cross off impossibilities aggressively — a day-month puzzle is solved by what can't go where. Month-length facts matter: February has 28/29 days; a schedule spanning Feb–Mar with "30-day month" clues pins March. Trap: "no two adjacent months" vs "no two consecutive days" — different constraints, re-read.

5.6 Comparison and blood-puzzle hybrids

Comparison puzzles ("tallest, oldest, heaviest" with multiple attributes) are won by a table with one column per attribute. Mark ties as unknown until confirmed. Hybrids (e.g. seating with professions) need two-slot notation per seat: person + attribute.

5.7 Time discipline

A 5-question puzzle set is worth 8–10 minutes in prelims. Read all clues before drawing; if two clues contradict your first placement, restart rather than patch. If stuck after one full pass, abandon — a puzzle with a missed negative clue ("not adjacent") eats time without reward.

6. Syllogism, Inequalities & Blood Relations

6.1 Syllogism: the four statement types

  • All A are B (universal affirmative): the A circle sits fully inside B.
  • Some A are B (particular affirmative): the circles overlap.
  • No A is B (universal negative): the circles are disjoint.
  • Some A are not B (particular negative): part of A lies outside B.

Three hard rules that decide nearly everything:

  1. No conclusion from two negative premises. "No A is B. Some C are not B." → nothing follows about A and C.
  2. No conclusion from two particular premises. "Some A are B. Some B are C." → nothing follows about A and C. (The overlap might be different B's.)
  3. The middle term must be distributed at least once — i.e. at least one premise must make a claim about all of the middle term. "All A are B. All C are B." gives nothing about A and C, because B is never fully covered.

Worked example: Statements: "All pens are books. Some books are red." Conclusions: (I) Some pens are red. (II) Some books are pens. (I) fails — the "some books" that are red may be different books from the pen-books (rule 2). (II) follows: "All pens are books" converts to "Some books are pens". Only II follows.

6.2 Possibility and "only" statements

Possibility conclusions ("Some A can be B" / "All A can be B") follow unless the statements make them impossible — a possibility conclusion fails only if it contradicts a definite negative. If the premises don't forbid it, possibility holds. This is the most misunderstood rule in syllogism.

"Only" statements: "Only A are B" = "All B are A" (and "Some B are A", "No non-A is B"). Treat "Only A are B" as reversed: "Only poets are dreamers" → All dreamers are poets.

6.3 Coded inequalities: symbol mechanics

Standard codes: P > Q means P greater than Q; P ≥ Q means greater than or equal; P = Q means equal; P ≠ Q means either greater or less (not equal); P ≤ Q, P < Q similarly. Chain the symbols: A > B = C > D → A > D is not definite (B = C, but A > B and C > D give A > D? Actually A > B = C means A > C, and C > D gives A > D — follows). The rule: moving along the chain, the weakest relation encountered is all you can claim; if any link is broken (< vs >, or ≥ with > mixed direction), no conclusion.

Worked example: Statements: A > B ≥ C = D. Conclusions: (I) A > D. (II) B = D. (I): A > B and B ≥ C = D → A > D follows (the strict > dominates). (II): B ≥ D, could be equal or greater — does not follow as a definite statement.

6.4 The either-or condition

"Either conclusion I or II follows" applies when: (1) both conclusions concern the same two elements; (2) both relations are possible but neither is definite; (3) the relations are complementary — together they cover all cases. Complementary pairs: (>, ≤), (<, ≥), (=, ≠), and the classic triple (>, <, =) when all three are possible.

Worked example: Statements: P = Q, R > Q. Conclusions: (I) P > R. (II) R > P. Since P = Q and R > Q, we know R > P definitely — so II follows outright, no either-or needed. Now change to: P ≥ Q, R ≥ Q. Conclusions: (I) P > R. (II) R > P. Neither is definite; but (III-style) the complementary pair (P > R, P ≤ R)… with the given options, check complementarity exactly: (I) P > R and (II) R ≥ P would be complementary. Trap: (P > R) vs (P < R) are not complementary — the equal case is missing.

6.5 Blood relations: the family-tree method

Draw generations top-down; mark gender with ♂/♀ or +/-; connect spouses with a double line, parent–child with a vertical line. Never assume gender: "sibling" ≠ brother, "child" ≠ son, "parent" ≠ father.

  • Pointing problems: "A man points to a photo and says: 'She is the daughter of my grandfather's only son.'" My grandfather's only son = my father. Father's daughter = my sister (or me, if the speaker is female — check the speaker's gender: "a man" → sister).
  • "Only" logic: "only son" means no brothers — but sisters may exist. "Only child" means neither.
  • Coded relations: expand symbols left to right, keeping the direction. "A + B − C" with + = father, − = sister: A is father of B, B is sister of C → A is father of C (B's sibling), so A is C's father. Draw as you decode; never decode the whole chain mentally.
  • Marriage links: spouses are the same generation; "mother-in-law's husband" = father-in-law; keep a small conversion table: spouse's father = father-in-law, sibling's child = nephew/niece.

Trap: "X is the brother of Y's father" — X could be Y's uncle or Y's father himself? No: brother of the father is the paternal uncle (father is not his own brother). But "X is the son of Y's grandfather" — if Y's grandfather has multiple sons, X could be Y's father or Y's uncle. Only sons are unique; plain "son" is not.

7. English Grammar & Error Detection

Bank prelims tests grammar through spotting-error sentences (choose the erroneous part) and mains adds sentence improvement, fillers and sentence correction. The same twelve rule families generate nearly every error-spotting question. Learn them as detection patterns, not as a textbook list.

1. Subject–verb agreement: find the true subject

The verb agrees with the grammatical subject, never with a noun sitting inside a prepositional phrase or parenthesis. In "The quality of the bank's loans is being reviewed", the subject is quality (singular), so is is correct despite the plural loans sitting nearby. Typical exam sentences deliberately place a plural noun between subject and verb: "The list of approved candidates has been published" — subject list, singular, hence has.

Special traps: "A number of" is plural (a number of employees are...) while "the number of" is singular (the number of complaints has...). Singular indefinite pronouns — each, either, neither, everyone, somebody — take singular verbs even when followed by of + plural: "Each of the officers was questioned." With either…or / neither…nor, the verb usually agrees with the nearer subject: "Neither the clerk nor the managers were present."

2. Tense consistency and time markers

Keep one narrative time-frame. Since marks a starting point (since March) and for marks a duration (for two years) — a favourite error: "He has been working here since three years" is wrong; for is correct. Use present perfect for an action linked to the present ("The RBI has cut the repo rate") and simple past for finished time ("The RBI cut the repo rate last December" — "has cut last December" would be wrong). After did/does/do, use the base verb: "He did not attend", never "did not attended".

3. Articles: sound, not spelling

Choose a/an by the first sound: an hour, an MBA, a university, a one-time settlement. Use the when the noun is specific, already identified, unique, or superlative: "the repo rate announced this week". Omit articles before plural/uncountable nouns used generally ("Banks need capital") and after verbs like appoint, elect, nominate when a title follows ("She was appointed branch manager").

4. Preposition collocations

Exams test fixed pairs, not logic: comply with, differ from, interested in, capable of, responsible for, accuse of, congratulate on, depend on/upon, refrain from, succeed in, aim at, apologise to (a person) for (a fault). Also: by a deadline (the payment is due by Friday) versus until for continuation (the branch remains open until 6 pm). Between for two distinct items, among for a group: "The bonus was divided among the staff."

5. Pronouns and reference

A pronoun must point clearly at one antecedent and match it in number and gender. "Every candidate must submit his or her form" — "their" with a singular antecedent is the planted error (formal bank English still penalises the singular they in spotting-error items). Avoid ambiguous reference: "Ravi told Sumit that he was late" leaves he undecided — exams frame the sentence so that the pronoun's target is unclear, and the correction clarifies it.

6. Misplaced and dangling modifiers

The opening phrase must logically modify the subject that follows: "After reviewing the file, the officer approved the loan" is correct; "After reviewing the file, the loan was approved" dangles — a loan cannot review a file. Only shifts meaning with position: "Only she approved the loan" (no one else did) vs "She approved only the loan" (nothing else).

7. Parallel structure

Items joined by and/or or presented as a list must share form: "The committee will assess, verify and report" — not "assess, verification and reporting". Correlative pairs join equivalent structures: "not only efficient but also secure". Comparisons must compare like with like: "The interest rate on savings accounts is lower than that on fixed deposits" — never compare a rate with a deposit directly.

Worked error-spotting drill

  • "One of the most important lesson (A) / taught to young bankers (B) / is to verify documents carefully (C) / before sanctioning a loan (D)." — Error in (A): "one of the most important lessons". "One of" is followed by a plural noun.
  • "Neither the manager (A) / nor the cashiers (B) / was aware (C) / of the new circular (D)." — Error in (C): the nearer subject cashiers is plural, so were.
  • "Had the applicant submitted (A) / the form in time, (B) / he would have been (C) / considered for the post (D)." — No error: this is a correct past-conditional ("had + past participle … would have + past participle").

Exam-day protocol

  • Read the sentence once for meaning, once scanning subject→verb→tense→article→preposition→modifier order.
  • In mains sentence-improvement, pick the option that fixes the grammar without changing the meaning; a grammatically smooth option that alters the facts is a trap.
  • "No error" is a valid answer — typically 1 in 4–5 items. Do not hunt errors that aren't there.

8. Vocabulary, Cloze Test & Reading Comprehension

Bank English rewards precise vocabulary: words you can place in the right sentence, not word lists you can recite. Three question families carry this section — vocabulary in context, cloze tests and reading comprehension — and they share one skill: choosing the option that fits the sentence's meaning, tone and grammar together.

Vocabulary in context: meaning lives in the sentence

A word can be correct by dictionary sense but wrong by tone or collocation. "The bank's prudent lending policy" — prudent fits a positive context; "cautious" would shade it toward timidity, "stingy" toward meanness. When stuck between two options, test each by substitution: read the sentence with the option inserted and check (a) dictionary sense, (b) positive/neutral/negative tone, (c) the preposition it demands (consistent with, not consistent to).

Common exam confusion pairs: adapt/adopt (adjust vs take up), elicit/illicit (draw out vs illegal), affect/effect (verb vs noun), complement/compliment (complete vs praise), principal/principle (main sum vs rule), assure/ensure/insure (promise a person / make certain / cover risk), judicial/judicious (of courts / wise). For roots and affixes, use them as clues, not proof: bene- (good: benefit, benevolent), mal- (bad: malaise, malicious), cred- (believe: credible, credulous), mit- (send: remit, transmit), -phobia (fear), circum- (around: circumvent). A root supports a guess but the sentence has the final vote.

Idioms and phrases most frequently tested

Cut corners (do cheap work), blessing in disguise, hit the nail on the head, once in a blue moon, piece of cake, spill the beans, beat around the bush, burn the midnight oil, by leaps and bounds, in hot water, throw in the towel, turn a blind eye, up in the air, white elephant, read between the lines, the ball is in your court, barking up the wrong tree. Learn each with one original example sentence — passive recognition fails under time pressure.

Cloze test: grammar first, then meaning

Procedure: (1) read the whole passage before touching a blank — tone and logical flow are decided there; (2) for each blank, eliminate by grammar first (does it need a verb, noun, preposition, conjunction?); (3) among grammatical options, choose by meaning and collocation; (4) re-read the completed passage for coherence.

Watch transition words — they advertise the logic: however, nevertheless, despite = contrast; moreover, furthermore, in addition = continuation; consequently, therefore, hence = cause–effect. A blank right after despite cannot take a word meaning "because". Pronoun reference is another favourite: the blank before its or they must introduce the matching noun.

Worked miniature: "Digital payments have grown ___ (A) leaps and bounds. ___, (B) cash still dominates rural trade. The gap ___ (C) infrastructure, not intent." — (A): by (fixed idiom by leaps and bounds); (B): However (contrast); (C): reflects — the gap reflects infrastructure; "is of" would be unidiomatic, "due to" would need is due to.

Reading comprehension: read for structure, not detail

On the first read, map the passage: issue → evidence → contrast → author's stance → conclusion. Most questions then resolve by location rather than memory. Question types and tactics:

  • Main idea / title: choose the option covering the whole passage. Broad options overreach (cover more than the passage claims); narrow ones miss the point.
  • Factual: return to the exact lines; the option that adds even one unverified word is wrong.
  • Inference: pick what must or most reasonably follow — supported but not stated. If it could be false, reject it.
  • Tone/attitude: derive from word choice (approving, critical, neutral, cautiously optimistic). Financial editorials typically run analytical or cautiously optimistic.
  • Vocabulary-in-passage: test by substitution in that sentence, not from your memorised list.

Speed method: read the questions first (not options), then read the passage hunting answers — factual questions become scanning tasks. Keep 8–10 minutes for a full RC set in prelims; never re-read the whole passage for one question.

Para jumbles: build mandatory pairs

Find the independent opening sentence (self-contained, no pronoun without antecedent, no however/therefore start). Then link mandatory pairs: pronoun after its noun, example after claim, contrast after position, chronology in order. A sentence with "this policy" must follow the sentence that names the policy. Verify the final order reads as one coherent argument.

9. Banking Awareness: RBI, Bank Types, NPA, Basel Norms, Digital Banking

Banking Awareness is the highest-ROI mains section: it is static, finite and repeated with predictable patterns. Master five blocks — the RBI, bank types, asset quality (NPA), Basel norms and digital banking — and 25–35 mains marks are essentially free.

9.1 The RBI — the central bank

Established 1 April 1935 under the RBI Act, 1934, on the Hilton Young Commission's recommendation; nationalised on 1 January 1949; headquarters Mumbai. Functions: sole currency issuer (except one-rupee coins/notes, issued by the Government of India), banker to the government, banker to banks, regulator of banks and NBFCs, manager of foreign exchange reserves, operator/regulator of payment systems, and monetary-policy authority. The Governor as of 2026 is Sanjay Malhotra (verified from RBI MPC announcements).

Monetary Policy Committee (MPC): 6 members — 3 from RBI (Governor + 2 officers) and 3 external members appointed by the government; meets at least 4 times a year, quorum 4; each member has one vote, the Governor has a casting vote. Target: CPI inflation of 4% with a ±2% band.

Policy-rate toolkit (rates verified as of mid-2026): Repo rate 5.25%, Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) 5.00%, Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) and Bank Rate 5.50%. Repo: RBI lends short-term to banks against eligible collateral (the LAF floor/ceiling corridor: SDF is the floor, MSF the ceiling). CRR = share of net demand and time liabilities kept as cash with RBI (no interest); SLR = share kept in liquid assets (cash, gold, approved securities) with the bank itself. Open Market Operations (buying/selling G-secs) adjust durable liquidity; the reverse repo era ended with the SDF replacing it as the floor.

9.3 NPA — asset quality and resolution

A loan becomes a Non-Performing Asset when interest or principal stays overdue for more than 90 days (term loans). NPA categories: Substandard (NPA for ≤ 12 months), Doubtful (substandard for > 12 months), Loss (identified as uncollectible). Banks must make provisions against NPAs; high NPAs erode capital and lending capacity.

Resolution framework: SARFAESI Act, 2002 lets secured creditors enforce security interest without court intervention; IBC, 2016 provides time-bound corporate insolvency resolution through the NCLT; Debt Recovery Tribunals handle recovery suits; NARCL (the "bad bank") aggregates large stressed assets.

9.4 Basel norms — capital, risk, liquidity

Basel norms are global standards from the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (Bank for International Settlements, Basel, Switzerland) covering how much capital banks must hold against risk.

  • Basel I (1988): introduced minimum capital against credit risk — Capital Adequacy Ratio (CRAR) of 8% of risk-weighted assets.
  • Basel II (2004): three pillars — (1) minimum capital requirements (credit + market + operational risk), (2) supervisory review, (3) market discipline/disclosure.
  • Basel III (post-2008): higher-quality capital and buffers — CET1 4.5%, Tier 1 6%, total CRAR 8%, plus Capital Conservation Buffer 2.5% (effective total 10.5%), countercyclical buffer, leverage ratio, and liquidity ratios: LCR (Liquidity Coverage Ratio — 30-day stress liquidity) and NSFR (Net Stable Funding Ratio — one-year stable funding).

India implemented Basel III with RBI-prescribed minimums; CRAR = (Tier 1 + Tier 2 capital) / Risk-Weighted Assets. Remember: Tier 1 = core capital (equity, disclosed reserves); Tier 2 = supplementary (revaluation reserves, subordinated debt).

9.5 Digital banking and payments

UPI (operated by NPCI) enables instant account-to-account transfers via VPA/mobile/QR. From 15 October 2026, a 0.4% Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) applies to specified person-to-merchant transactions above ₹2,000 (person-to-person and sub-₹2,000 merchant payments stay free; cap of ₹300 for transactions of ₹75,000+; essential thin-margin sectors such as railways, telecom, insurance and fuel attract a flat ₹5). UPI is accepted in 11 countries. IMPS = 24×7 instant interbank transfer; NEFT = half-hourly batch settlement, 24×7; RTGS = real-time gross (per-transaction) settlement for high value; AEPS = Aadhaar-based basic banking via business correspondents; NACH handles bulk debits/credits (EMIs, salaries, DBT).

e₹ (digital rupee / CBDC): RBI's sovereign digital currency in retail pilot; an offline mode announced at Global Fintech Fest 2025 allows up to ₹50,000/day (max 20 transactions/day, wallet balance cap ₹1 lakh) via NFC — designed for low-connectivity areas. FASTag, BBPS (Bharat Bill Payment System), ONDC and account aggregators complete the digital rails. Fraud defence: banks never need your PIN/OTP to receive money — that is the phishing test.

  • The "no income-tax change" headline coexists with big compliance changes (new Act, deadlines, TCS) — questions mix both.

12.3 Digital payments and fintech

  • UPI MDR (effective 15 Oct 2026): 0.4% on specified person-to-merchant transactions above ₹2,000; P2P stays free; merchant payments ≤₹2,000 stay free; cap of ₹300 for transactions ≥₹75,000; essential sectors (railways, telecom, insurance, fuel) pay a flat ₹5 above ₹2,000. RBI backed it on 15 Sept 2026 as a sustainability measure. UPI accepted in 11 countries.
  • Offline e₹ (CBDC): announced at Global Fintech Fest 2025 (Oct 2025, Mumbai) — NFC-based offline digital-rupee payments up to ₹50,000/day, max 20 transactions/day, wallet balance cap ₹1 lakh; targets low-connectivity regions.

12.5 Economy and markets

  • Fiscal consolidation path: 4.4% (FY26) → 4.3% (FY27) deficit; debt-to-GDP falling 56.1% → 55.6%.
  • Gross market borrowings FY27 estimated ₹17.2 lakh crore; net tax receipts ₹28.7 lakh crore.
  • Safe-harbour limit for IT services raised to ₹2,000 crore at a uniform 15.5% margin; tax holiday till 2047 for foreign cloud/data-centre firms.
  • 20 new waterways to be operationalised; coastal cargo promotion scheme; Dankuni freight corridor.

12.6 How to use this chapter

Exam-room strategy

The most useful plan is one you have already rehearsed in full mocks.

Use a two-pass method

First solve direct questions. Mark lengthy but solvable items for a second pass. Do not let one puzzle consume a section.

Protect accuracy

If two options remain and you cannot prove one, move on. Negative marking changes the value of a doubtful attempt.

Read the stem twice

Words such as “not”, “except”, “least” and “incorrect” reverse the task. Circle them mentally before calculating.

Analyze every mock

Classify errors as concept, calculation, reading, selection or time. The repair depends on the category.

Build current affairs by theme

Group facts under banking, economy, national, international, appointments, awards and reports. Revise through questions.

For descriptive papers

Use a clear opening, two or three developed points, and a balanced conclusion. Practice typing within a timer.
